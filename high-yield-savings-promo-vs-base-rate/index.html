High-yield savings: promo rate vs ongoing rate

Updated Nov 10, 2026 · 14 options with a confirmed rate shown on this page

Banks often advertise a higher APY that lasts a few months, or that you only get if you meet a condition. The rate that remains after the boost ends, or if you miss the condition, can be lower. This page lists both figures for the savings and broker-cash accounts we track, when we have them, and it ranks the accounts by the ongoing rate — not by the headline.

Rates change. Confirm the current APY, the length of any boost, and the conditions on the provider's site before you move money. Not financial advice.

Why the two numbers are not interchangeable

A promotional APY is usually limited by time, by a new-customer rule, by a required deposit, or by a bundled checking account. An ongoing APY is what the account lists after that condition no longer applies. Comparing a six-month promo at one bank with an ongoing rate at another mixes two different offers.

Where we know both ends and the boost has a stated length, we also show a simple first-year blend: half a year at the promo rate and half a year at the ongoing rate. That math assumes the ongoing rate does not change, the boost lasts the stated term, and there is no balance cap. Those assumptions often fail, so the blend is an illustration, not a forecast.

We do not invent an ongoing rate when the provider's page, as we read it, does not publish one.

A worked example

Take Bask Bank Savings, where both ends and a term are in our notes: 4.30% for up to 6 months, then 3.90% if the listed base rate is still 3.90%.

If you kept $10,000 in the account for 12 months, and both rates held as written, the first-year blend of 4.10% would be about $410 of interest before tax (0.041 × $10,000). Six months at 4.30% and six months at 3.90%, with simple weighting and no compounding, is the same 4.10%. Compounding, a cap on the boosted balance, or a change in the base rate would change the result.

By comparison, EverBank Performance Savings is listed at 4.20% APY with no promo in our notes. That 4.20% is already above Bask's 4.10% blend, and it is below Bask's 4.30% headline. Which figure you compare depends on how long you expect to keep the money there, and on whether you will open the bundled checking account Bask requires for the boost.

This is an illustration using this week's figures, not a recommendation of either account.

What we leave out

  • We do not rank by the headline promo. The table is ordered to keep the boosted and qualification offers together at the top, then the straight ongoing accounts.
  • We do not treat a qualification rate (Happen Bank's deposit rule, Varo's requirements, SoFi's direct deposit) as the same thing as a time-limited promo.
  • We do not fill in a post-promo APY when the provider's page, as we read it, does not publish one.
  • Raisin is a marketplace, not a bank. FDIC insurance, when it applies, is through the partner bank, up to applicable limits.

What the risk labels mean

FDIC
A bank deposit. If the bank fails, the FDIC covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Broker "cash sweep" accounts spread money across partner banks to raise that limit.
SIPC
A security held at a brokerage, such as a money market fund. SIPC protects you if the brokerage fails and your assets go missing (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash). It does not protect against the fund losing value. Government money funds aim for a stable $1 share price, which is not guaranteed.
Treasury-backed
A direct loan to the US government (T-bills). There is no insurance, and none is needed in the usual sense: you are paid by the US Treasury. If you sell before maturity the price can move a little.
Not insured
Crypto stablecoin rewards (USDC). There is no FDIC or SIPC coverage. You depend on the exchange, the stablecoin issuer, and rules that are still being written.
FDIC / SIPC
A mix. For example, part of the balance is held as brokerage cash (SIPC) and the rest is swept to banks (FDIC).
