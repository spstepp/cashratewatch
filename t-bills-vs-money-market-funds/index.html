T-bills vs money market funds

Updated Nov 10, 2026 · 14 options with a confirmed rate shown on this page

Treasury bills and money market funds are both ways to keep cash in short-term government or government-backed debt, or in a fund that holds that kind of debt. The differences are how long your money is committed, how you are taxed, and what the risk label means.

This week's numbers: the latest 52-week T-bill auction paid 4.496% (bond-equivalent, auction Oct 27, 2026). The highest money market fund we list is Vanguard Treasury Money Market (VUSXX) at 3.97% (7-day SEC yield as of Nov 9, 2026). Those figures are not directly interchangeable — one is an auction rate for a bill you hold to a date, the other is a recent annualized fund yield that can move.

Rates change. Confirm current yields, minimums and fund documents with the provider, or the latest Treasury auction results, before you move money. Not financial advice.

State tax can tip the balance

T-bill interest is exempt from state and local income tax. A simple way to compare a T-bill with a fully taxable yield is: T-bill rate ÷ (1 − state tax rate). If you pay 5% state income tax, this week's 13-week bill at 4.159% matches a fully taxable yield of about 4.38%. This week's 52-week bill at 4.496% matches about 4.73%.

5% is an example, not your rate. This leaves out federal tax; if you do not deduct state income tax on your federal return, the break-even taxable yield is a little higher.

A Treasury money market fund may also get a partial or full state exemption, which narrows that gap. The share that qualifies is published after the year ends and differs by fund and by state, so we do not apply a fund-level tax adjustment here.

In a state with no income tax, there is no state-tax difference, and you are left comparing the raw yields, the term, and access to the cash.

Which fits

  • Cash you may need on any business day is simpler in a money market fund, especially if it is already at that brokerage as a settlement fund.
  • Cash you can leave until a known date may earn more in a T-bill at this week's auctions, particularly if you pay state income tax. The 4-week bill at 3.956% is slightly below VUSXX's 3.97%, so the shortest bill is not ahead of the top fund this week on the raw number.
  • Prime funds such as SWVXX can pay more than some Treasury funds and carry a little more credit risk. Treasury and government funds are narrower.
  • If your cash already sits at Fidelity, Schwab or Vanguard, buying that broker's fund avoids a transfer. Buying a T-bill there is also possible; TreasuryDirect is not the only way.

What the risk labels mean

FDIC
A bank deposit. If the bank fails, the FDIC covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Broker "cash sweep" accounts spread money across partner banks to raise that limit.
SIPC
A security held at a brokerage, such as a money market fund. SIPC protects you if the brokerage fails and your assets go missing (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash). It does not protect against the fund losing value. Government money funds aim for a stable $1 share price, which is not guaranteed.
Treasury-backed
A direct loan to the US government (T-bills). There is no insurance, and none is needed in the usual sense: you are paid by the US Treasury. If you sell before maturity the price can move a little.
Not insured
Crypto stablecoin rewards (USDC). There is no FDIC or SIPC coverage. You depend on the exchange, the stablecoin issuer, and rules that are still being written.
FDIC / SIPC
A mix. For example, part of the balance is held as brokerage cash (SIPC) and the rest is swept to banks (FDIC).
