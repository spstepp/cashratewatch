T-bills vs high-yield savings

Updated Oct 27, 2026 · 11 options with a confirmed rate shown on this page

T-bills and high-yield savings accounts both pay close to short-term interest rates. The differences are taxes, access to your money, and how long the rate lasts. Here is how they compare with this week's numbers.

State tax can tip the balance

T-bill interest is taxed federally but is exempt from state and local income tax. Savings interest is taxed at both levels. If you pay 5% state income tax, the 13-week T-bill at 4.185% matches a savings account paying about 4.41%. In a state with no income tax, there is no tax difference. At this week's rates, the 13-week T-bill's 4.185% works out to about 4.41% in a taxable savings account at a 5% state income tax, above the top savings rate we list (4.30%).

How we get this: T-bill rate ÷ (1 − state tax rate). 5% is an example rate, not your rate. This leaves out federal tax; if you don't deduct state income tax on your federal return, the break-even savings rate is a little higher.

Which fits

  • Money you may need any day, such as an emergency fund, is simpler in savings.
  • Money you can leave for a set number of weeks or months may earn more in T-bills, especially if you pay state income tax. This week the T-bill rates run from 4.068% (4-week) to 4.616% (52-week).
  • Promotional savings rates can end. Check the notes under each account.

What the risk labels mean

FDIC
A bank deposit. If the bank fails, the FDIC covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Broker "cash sweep" accounts spread money across partner banks to raise that limit.
SIPC
A security held at a brokerage, such as a money market fund. SIPC protects you if the brokerage fails and your assets go missing (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash). It does not protect against the fund losing value. Government money funds aim for a stable $1 share price, which is not guaranteed.
Treasury-backed
A direct loan to the US government (T-bills). There is no insurance, and none is needed in the usual sense: you are paid by the US Treasury. If you sell before maturity the price can move a little.
Not insured
Crypto stablecoin rewards (USDC). There is no FDIC or SIPC coverage. You depend on the exchange, the stablecoin issuer, and rules that are still being written.
FDIC / SIPC
A mix. For example, part of the balance is held as brokerage cash (SIPC) and the rest is swept to banks (FDIC).
