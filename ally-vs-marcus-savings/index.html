Ally vs Marcus: which savings account pays more?

Ally Bank and Marcus by Goldman Sachs are two of the best-known online savings accounts. Both are FDIC-insured, both have no monthly fees, and both rates are variable. This week Marcus pays more.

Which one fits

  • You want the higher rate between the two: Marcus, at 3.50% vs 3.20% this week.
  • You like splitting savings into goals inside one account: Ally's buckets do that while paying interest on the whole balance.
  • You move large sums in and out quickly: Marcus advertises same-day transfers up to $100,000.
  • You want the most interest overall: neither tops our table. On our Nov 17 check, Western Alliance Bank via Raisin paid 4.35% (a 90-day rate lock for new Raisin users), EverBank Performance Savings 4.20%, and Bask Bank 3.90%. Before you chase a headline rate, read promo rate vs base rate.

Ally and Marcus vs a money market fund

If your money is going to sit for a while, a Treasury money market fund at a broker can pay more than either bank. VMFXX yielded 3.88% on our last check. Money funds aren't FDIC-insured, though. See high-yield savings vs money market fund for the trade-offs.

Questions

Is Marcus better than Ally?

On rate, yes this week: 3.50% vs 3.20%. Both have no monthly fees and FDIC insurance. Ally's draw is its budgeting tools; Marcus's is speed on large transfers.

Do Ally and Marcus rates change?

Yes. Both are variable and can change at any time after you open the account. We recheck them every week.

Is my money safe at Ally or Marcus?

Both are FDIC-insured banks, which covers deposits up to the legal limit per depositor, per bank, per ownership category.

What the risk labels mean

FDIC
A bank deposit. If the bank fails, the FDIC covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Broker "cash sweep" accounts spread money across partner banks to raise that limit.
SIPC
A security held at a brokerage, such as a money market fund. SIPC protects you if the brokerage fails and your assets go missing (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash). It does not protect against the fund losing value. Government money funds aim for a stable $1 share price, which is not guaranteed.
Treasury-backed
A direct loan to the US government (T-bills). There is no insurance, and none is needed in the usual sense: you are paid by the US Treasury. If you sell before maturity the price can move a little.
Not insured
Crypto stablecoin rewards (USDC). There is no FDIC or SIPC coverage. You depend on the exchange, the stablecoin issuer, and rules that are still being written.
FDIC / SIPC
A mix. For example, part of the balance is held as brokerage cash (SIPC) and the rest is swept to banks (FDIC).
