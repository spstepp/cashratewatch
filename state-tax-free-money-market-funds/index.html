State-tax-free money market funds: VUSXX, SNSXX and what you keep after state tax

Money market fund yields look close on paper. After state income tax they can drift apart, because interest from US Treasury securities is exempt from state income tax and some funds hold far more Treasuries than others. If you live in a state with an income tax, the fund with the highest headline yield may not leave you with the most.

These after-tax figures use this week's 7-day yields and each fund's 2025 tax data. Not tax or financial advice. Your state's rules, your own rate and your tax software decide the real number.

What the table says

  • Among money funds, Treasury-only funds win once state tax is counted. At a 5% state rate, VUSXX keeps all 3.98%, ahead of SWVXX's 3.76% headline yield, which drops to about 3.57% because none of its income is from Treasuries.
  • "Treasury" in the name isn't enough. Funds that hold a lot of repurchase agreements (repos) backed by Treasuries don't pass the exemption through, because repo income is generally taxable by states. SNOXX reported 31.30% for 2025 and FZFXX 61.52%. Neither met the CA/CT/NY test.
  • The default sweep fund may be the weakest choice. SPAXX, Fidelity's default core fund, reported 50.90% and failed the CA/CT/NY test, so in those states all of its dividends are state-taxable.
  • A top savings account can still win in a no-income-tax state. With no state tax, the HYSA's 4.20% and a direct T-bill beat every fund here. The state-tax edge only matters where you pay state tax.

The California, Connecticut and New York 50% rule

Most states let you exclude the Treasury share of a fund's dividends. California, Connecticut and New York add a gate: the fund must hold at least 50% of its assets in qualifying US government obligations at the end of every quarter of its tax year. Miss it once and none of that year's dividends are exempt in those three states. Each fund family marks which funds passed in its annual tax supplement.

How to claim it

You don't get the exemption automatically. Your 1099-DIV shows total ordinary dividends in Box 1a. Multiply that by the fund's percentage for the year (from the fund family's tax supplement, usually posted in January or February) and subtract the result on your state return. Tax software usually asks for it as "US government interest" or "US obligations." Fund families post new percentages each year, and they move: a fund's mix of Treasuries and repos changes.

VUSXX vs VMFXX in one line

Both are Vanguard, both met the CA/CT/NY test for 2025. VUSXX paid 3.98% this week with 100% Treasury income; VMFXX paid 3.88% with 66.61%. In a state with income tax, VUSXX comes out ahead this week on both counts.

What the risk labels mean

FDIC
A bank deposit. If the bank fails, the FDIC covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Broker "cash sweep" accounts spread money across partner banks to raise that limit.
SIPC
A security held at a brokerage, such as a money market fund. SIPC protects you if the brokerage fails and your assets go missing (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash). It does not protect against the fund losing value. Government money funds aim for a stable $1 share price, which is not guaranteed.
Treasury-backed
A direct loan to the US government (T-bills). There is no insurance, and none is needed in the usual sense: you are paid by the US Treasury. If you sell before maturity the price can move a little.
Not insured
Crypto stablecoin rewards (USDC). There is no FDIC or SIPC coverage. You depend on the exchange, the stablecoin issuer, and rules that are still being written.
FDIC / SIPC
A mix. For example, part of the balance is held as brokerage cash (SIPC) and the rest is swept to banks (FDIC).
