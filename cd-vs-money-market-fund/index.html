Rate lock vs floating yield

A money market fund's yield isn't fixed. It moves with short-term rates, which follow the Fed. When the Fed cuts, fund yields tend to fall soon after; when it raises rates, they climb.

A CD locks its APY for the term if you hold it to maturity. If you think rates will fall over the next year, a 4.50% 1-year CD keeps paying that rate. If you think they'll rise, the fund can follow them up and the CD can't.

Getting to your money

  • Money market funds: you can sell on any business day with no penalty.
  • CDs: taking money out before the term ends costs a penalty. At Bread Savings, the 3-month CD charges 90 days of simple interest. On $10,000 at 3.95% that's about $97. Safety

CDs at an FDIC member bank are deposits, insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category (FDIC).

Money market funds are not FDIC-insured. They aim to keep a $1 share price, which is not guaranteed. At a brokerage, SIPC covers missing assets if the broker fails, not a drop in the fund's value. VUSXX, VMFXX and SPAXX are government or Treasury funds, which hold US government debt. SWVXX is a prime fund, which can also hold short-term corporate debt.

Taxes

CD interest is taxed federally and by your state, if it has an income tax. Money market fund dividends are taxed federally. Part or all of the income from government and Treasury funds may be exempt from state income tax. The rules differ by state, so check the fund's annual tax information. Minimums

  • Money market funds: SPAXX $0; SWVXX $0; VMFXX $3,000; VUSXX $3,000. Vanguard waives the minimum for VMFXX when it is your brokerage settlement fund.
  • CDs: Bread Savings $1,500, Marcus $500, EverBank $1,000.

Which one fits

  • You might need the money soon: a money market fund. You can sell any business day with no penalty.
  • You won't touch it until a set date and want a rate that won't change: a CD.
  • You think rates will fall: a CD locks today's rate. A fund's yield would drift down with them.
  • You want FDIC insurance: a CD at an FDIC member bank, or a high-yield savings account.
  • You pay state income tax: part of a Treasury or government fund's income may be exempt from state tax. CD interest isn't.

Can a money market fund's yield drop?

Yes. A money market fund's yield moves with short-term interest rates, so it can fall when the Fed cuts. A CD's APY is fixed for the term if you hold it to maturity.

Is a money market fund FDIC-insured like a CD?

No. CDs at an FDIC member bank are insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Money market funds are not FDIC-insured. They aim to keep a $1 share price, which is not guaranteed. At a brokerage, SIPC covers missing assets if the broker fails, not a drop in the fund's value.

How fast can I get my money out?

What the risk labels mean

FDIC
A bank deposit. If the bank fails, the FDIC covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Broker "cash sweep" accounts spread money across partner banks to raise that limit.
SIPC
A security held at a brokerage, such as a money market fund. SIPC protects you if the brokerage fails and your assets go missing (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash). It does not protect against the fund losing value. Government money funds aim for a stable $1 share price, which is not guaranteed.
Treasury-backed
A direct loan to the US government (T-bills). There is no insurance, and none is needed in the usual sense: you are paid by the US Treasury. If you sell before maturity the price can move a little.
Not insured
Crypto stablecoin rewards (USDC). There is no FDIC or SIPC coverage. You depend on the exchange, the stablecoin issuer, and rules that are still being written.
FDIC / SIPC
A mix. For example, part of the balance is held as brokerage cash (SIPC) and the rest is swept to banks (FDIC).
